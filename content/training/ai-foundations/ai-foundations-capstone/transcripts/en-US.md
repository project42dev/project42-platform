## Welcome: Welcome And Outcomes

Welcome to the AI Foundations capstone. This is not a contest for the most impressive output. It is a demonstration that you can choose a useful low-risk objective, plan and execute an AI-assisted workflow, preserve evidence without expanding privacy risk, verify every acceptance criterion, recover from failure, and hand the work to another person. Passing requires both knowledge and applied mastery evidence. A polished result without traceable boundaries and checks is not complete.

## Narration: Bounded Objective Explanation

Start with an objective brief. Name the person or group who needs the result and the decision or task it supports. Define one reviewable deliverable, a deadline or time box, and acceptance criteria that can produce direct evidence. List what is out of scope. Classify the data before it enters any AI service or tool. Remove credentials and personal, confidential, licensed, or restricted material the task does not require. Identify authoritative sources and distinguish read access, reversible internal changes, external actions, spending, permission changes, and destructive effects. State which actions are prohibited and which require exact approval. Choose a task small enough to complete and verify safely: a bounded research note, permitted document transformation, low-risk code correction, approved data analysis, or paper design of a tool workflow. The capstone measures control and evidence, not operational consequence.

- <https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework>

## Demonstration: Capstone Choice Demonstration

Compare two proposed capstones. The first says, build an autonomous system that fixes production incidents. It has broad authority, real customer impact, unclear completion, and dangerous recovery. Reject it. The second says, use an approved synthetic incident packet to produce a one-page triage draft that maps every claim to the packet, identifies unknowns, proposes no production action, and passes a factuality, accessibility, and escalation checklist. The deliverable is bounded, the evidence is available, and execution is low risk. Its acceptance criteria can be observed. A later team could use the evidence to decide whether to test a controlled tool workflow, but the capstone does not borrow that authority. Good scope preserves meaningful reasoning while removing unnecessary consequence.

## Narration: Workflow Plan Explanation

Build a workflow plan another person can follow. Separate instructions, trusted evidence, untrusted input, constraints, and the output contract. Identify the provider or tool surface from task requirements rather than habit. Record relevant versions and allowed data. Break the work into observable steps with an expected artifact or check after each step. Add checkpoints before external, expensive, destructive, privacy-sensitive, or permission-changing actions. Define what happens when evidence conflicts, a required source is unavailable, structured output fails, a tool times out, or an acceptance check fails. State turn, time, cost, and retry limits. Name the final stop and human handoff. Preserve prompts and material settings, but do not request or store hidden reasoning. A reproducible plan exposes inputs, actions, and decisions; it does not need access to private internal model processes.

## Narration: Evidence Log Explanation

Execute in small observable steps and keep an evidence log. Record the objective and plan version, source titles and stable locations, access dates, permitted input descriptions or safe digests, material prompts, tool requests and results, output versions, timestamps, checkpoints, approvals, and decisions needed for reproduction. Link each entry to an acceptance criterion or recovery need. Preserve raw or canonical inputs separately from generated transformations when policy permits. Do not store credentials, access tokens, hidden reasoning, unrestricted private data, or full content when a safe reference or digest is sufficient. If a tool returns untrusted instructions, keep them as evidence without allowing them to rewrite the plan. If the plan changes, record what changed and which evidence justified it. The evidence log should let a reviewer trace the result while keeping the privacy footprint no larger than the task requires.

## Narration: Verification And Recovery Explanation

Verify every acceptance criterion independently. Open authoritative sources and confirm claim scope. Run deterministic tests or calculations. Inspect the target artifact or system directly. Check meaning, accessibility, privacy, permissions, and prohibited side effects. Do not ask the same model whether its work is correct and count that as independent evidence. Record pass, fail, unknown, or not applicable with the exact supporting artifact. Then demonstrate recovery. Use a real failure encountered during safe work, or deliberately simulate a likely failure without creating harmful effects. Preserve the failure, stop condition, diagnosis, smallest correction, rerun, neighboring regression checks, rollback or escalation, and final state. A passing final version does not erase the failed attempt. Recovery evidence shows that you can detect and control error rather than merely avoid discussing it.

## Feedback: Missing Safety Evidence Feedback

The capstone cannot pass from the final report alone. Correctness and source evidence may earn supported points, but safety, reproducibility, recovery, and communication require their named artifacts. A self-score is not evidence. Return the submission for the minimum missing records: permitted-data classification and redactions, authority and approval boundaries, verification and recovery evidence, reviewer-ready handoff, reflection, and criterion-by-criterion evidence mapping. Then rescore. If you accepted ninety percent because the report was accurate, revise the rule: applied mastery includes how the result was produced, checked, controlled, and transferred—not only how it reads.

Correct feedback: You refused an unsupported passing score and required traceable safety, reproducibility, recovery, and handoff evidence before rescoring.

Retry feedback: Map every rubric criterion to its required artifacts. Accuracy alone cannot supply evidence for data handling, recovery, or handoff.

## Narration: Recovery Simulation Explanation

If no natural failure occurs, simulate one safely and honestly. Remove one required source from a research synthesis, corrupt one field in a copy of structured output, run a boundary input against a local test, or model a tool timeout on paper without performing an external write. State that the failure was simulated. Show how the workflow detects it, stops dependent work, preserves evidence, identifies the responsible layer, and chooses correction, retry, rollback, or escalation. Rerun the original criteria after repair. Do not fabricate a production incident, manipulate real learner records, send test messages to actual people, or create a failure that needs authority outside the capstone. The purpose is to demonstrate recovery competence, not to manufacture drama.

Sources:

- <https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework>

## Narration: Rubric And Handoff Explanation

Package five required artifacts: the objective and scope brief; prompt or workflow plan; source, tool, and result evidence; verification and recovery record; and reflection with reviewer-ready handoff. Score five equally weighted criteria. Correctness asks whether the deliverable meets its objective and criteria. Evidence asks whether claims and decisions map to authority or observation. Safety asks whether data, permissions, approvals, and consequences stayed bounded. Reproducibility asks whether another person can repeat the workflow and understand recovery. Communication asks whether the handoff separates facts, generated suggestions, decisions, uncertainty, limitations, and next steps. Award points only when the required evidence exists. Record reviewer feedback and preserve revisions. Completion requires at least eighty percent on the knowledge check and at least eighty percent on the capstone rubric. Neither substitutes for the other.
