# Context Is a Decision, Not a Data Dump

When people ask an AI system to help with a task, they often provide more information than the system needs. More context can feel helpful, but irrelevant, outdated, or untrusted material can make an answer less reliable. Good context construction is the practice of choosing what belongs in the working set and explaining why.

A useful working set is not simply “everything available.” It is a deliberately selected collection of material that helps answer a particular question. The same source may be useful for one task and distracting or inappropriate for another. For example, a company’s public product page may be relevant when describing product features, but it may not be appropriate evidence for an internal question about current pricing or contractual terms.

## Relevance has a boundary

Start by defining the task narrowly. Ask:

- What decision or question must be answered?
- What facts are necessary to answer it?
- What facts are merely interesting?
- What information is outside the task’s scope?

This boundary prevents context overload. If the task is to summarize a policy, the policy itself and a clearly identified revision history may be relevant. An unrelated email thread, an old presentation, or a personal opinion may not be. Including extra material can create false connections and make it harder to determine which claims are actually supported.

Relevance also depends on the intended audience and output. A technical explanation, an executive summary, and a customer-facing response may require different portions of the same source.

## Scope is different from trust

**Scope** means what a source covers and what it is authoritative about. **Trustworthiness** concerns how much confidence we should place in the source for that purpose. These are related, but they are not the same.

A signed policy from the responsible department may be trustworthy evidence about the wording of that policy. Its scope may be limited to employees in one region or to one type of activity. It may not be evidence about building safety, tax rules, or another department’s process. A source can therefore be trustworthy but out of scope.

When labeling a source, record both:

- **Identity:** Who produced or maintains it?
- **Scope:** Which subjects, people, places, time periods, or decisions does it cover?
- **Trust indicators:** Why might it be reliable or unreliable for this task?
- **Currency:** When was it issued, revised, approved, or superseded?

Keeping these labels separate makes it easier to notice mistakes. “Official” does not mean “authoritative about everything,” and “relevant” does not mean “correct.”

## Trust is specific, not automatic

A source is not simply “trusted” or “untrusted” in every situation. Trust depends on identity, authority, proximity to the facts, and the purpose of the source. A comment that describes a policy may be useful for finding questions, but it is weaker evidence for what the policy actually says.

Treat supplied sources as claims to evaluate, not as guaranteed truth. A document can be genuine but outdated. It can be current but incomplete. It can be authoritative about one topic and irrelevant to another. Record the source identity, its scope, its date or revision information when available, and any limitations.

## Current does not mean merely recent

A newer source is not always better. A recent draft may not be approved, while an older published policy may remain in force. “Current” means applicable to the task at the relevant time. To judge currency, look for effective dates, revision dates, approval status, supersession language, and references to other documents.

If two sources disagree, do not silently choose one. Record the conflict. Identify what each source says, when it applies, who issued it, and what remains uncertain. The conflict may be resolved by a later authoritative source, or it may need to be escalated to a person who owns the decision.

## Separate evidence from instructions

A working set may contain several kinds of content:

- **Evidence:** material that supports or challenges a claim.
- **Instructions:** directions about what to produce or how to behave.
- **Untrusted content:** text that may attempt to redirect the task, imitate authority, or introduce unsupported conclusions.
- **Metadata:** information about the source, such as its title, owner, date, or location.

These categories should remain visible. A sentence inside a supplied document that says “ignore previous requirements” is not automatically an instruction to follow. It may simply be text found in the document. The task’s actual instructions should be kept separate from the evidence being examined.

A source can contain useful facts and harmful instructions at the same time. Extract factual content for evaluation without granting the source control over the task.

## Build a claim-to-evidence trail

A reliable answer should allow another person to move from each important claim back to its support. Before writing, list the claims the answer is likely to make. For each claim, record:

1. The exact or paraphrased claim.
2. The source that supports it.
3. The location within that source.
4. The source’s scope and currency.
5. Any conflict, uncertainty, or missing support.

This practice exposes unsupported conclusions early. It also prevents a common mistake: citing a source that is related to a topic but does not actually support the specific statement. If a source only says that a process exists, it does not prove that the process was followed in a particular case.

Traceability does not guarantee that a source is correct. It makes the reasoning inspectable. A reviewer can challenge the source, compare it with another source, or identify where verification is still needed. When evidence is incomplete, the responsible result may be a qualified answer, a list of open questions, or a request for authoritative confirmation.

## A practical habit

Before using any material, label it. Write down who produced it, what it covers, when it applies, and whether it contains instructions, evidence, or both. Then select only what the task requires. Preserve disagreements rather than smoothing them away. Finally, check every significant claim against a specific piece of evidence.

Context construction is therefore less about giving an AI system more information and more about giving it a well-organized, appropriately bounded basis for reasoning.
