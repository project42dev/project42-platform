# Writing and Transformation as a Controlled Workflow

A writing request often sounds simple: “Turn this report into a short announcement,” or “Rewrite these notes for a general audience.” The difficult part is not producing fluent sentences. It is preserving what the source supports while changing the form, audience, length, or tone.

Think of transformation as **controlled change**. Some properties may change:

- length,
- format,
- reading level,
- organization,
- tone,
- amount of explanation.

Other properties should remain stable unless the brief explicitly authorizes a change:

- names,
- dates,
- numbers,
- claims,
- qualifications,
- uncertainty,
- attribution,
- scope.

For example:

> Source: “The pilot involved 24 participants and lasted six weeks. The results suggest a possible improvement, but the study did not include a comparison group.”

A safe plain-language transformation is:

> “Twenty-four people took part in a six-week pilot. The results suggest the program may have helped, but the study did not compare participants with another group.”

The vocabulary and sentence structure changed. The number, duration, cautious wording, and limitation stayed the same. An unsafe version would say, “The six-week pilot proved the program works.” It changes a suggestion into proof and removes an important limitation.

## Make the audience specific

“General audience” is a starting point, not a complete audience description. Ask what readers already know, what they need to do with the information, and what could make the text difficult to understand.

For example:

- Residents may need a short explanation of an unfamiliar term.
- Staff members may already know the terminology but need exact dates.
- Decision-makers may need limitations and evidence before acting.
- Readers using translation or screen-reading tools may benefit from short sentences, clear headings, and text alternatives.

A useful test is to imagine one reader asking, “What does this mean for me?” The answer helps determine what context to include. Do not add background facts simply to make the piece feel complete. If the source does not provide the needed context, identify it as an open question.

## Treat the brief as a control document

A writing brief makes a vague request testable. It should identify:

1. **Audience:** who will read the output and what they need.
2. **Purpose:** whether the piece informs, instructs, summarizes, persuades, or supports a decision.
3. **Source of truth:** the approved material that supports factual claims, including its title, date, version, or owner when available.
4. **Acceptance criteria:** observable conditions for approval.

A purpose can change what counts as a good transformation. A neutral public update should not become a recommendation. A set of instructions should not become a promotional summary. If the purpose is unclear, pause before drafting.

A brief may authorize a style, structure, or emphasis, but it does not automatically authorize new facts. “Highlight our innovation” may allow a heading that draws attention to an approved feature. It does not authorize calling the feature “industry-leading” unless evidence and approval support that claim.

## Build an evidence trail

Before drafting, list the source-backed points you expect to use. Record where each point appears and how it will be treated. This creates an evidence trail between the source and the output.

Classify each proposed point as:

- **Supported:** directly stated in the approved source.
- **Transformed:** expressed differently without changing its meaning.
- **Needs review:** unclear, incomplete, or dependent on an interpretation.
- **Unsupported:** not supported by the source and not specifically authorized with evidence.

Brief instructions about tone or emphasis do not turn unsupported content into fact. If a detail is missing, remove it, use a neutral placeholder, label it as unverified, or ask the content owner for evidence.

Pay special attention to numbers, dates, quotations, names, outcomes, and cause-and-effect language. “After” describes sequence. “Because” describes causation. These are not interchangeable.

## Review with different questions

A polished paragraph can still fail in several ways. Review it from separate perspectives:

- **Meaning:** Are the claim, scope, uncertainty, and limitations preserved?
- **Evidence:** Can each factual statement be traced to the source?
- **Voice:** Is the tone suitable without exaggeration or accidental endorsement?
- **Accessibility:** Are headings descriptive, sentences manageable, abbreviations explained, and essential information available as text?
- **Disclosure:** Is any AI or human contribution described accurately and as required?

Some checks need human judgment. For example, neutral writing does not mean removing every adjective. It means avoiding language that adds unsupported praise, pressure, or certainty. When a judgment is borderline, name the question, compare the sentence with the brief, and ask the content owner or reviewer to decide. Record the decision rather than silently guessing.

## Recover from drift

Drift occurs when a draft gradually changes audience, becomes more confident than the source, loses a caveat, or gains invented specificity. Recovery is easier when you stop early:

1. Stop extending the questionable draft.
2. Return to the brief and the approved source.
3. Mark unsupported, ambiguous, or changed passages.
4. Restore omitted qualifications and attribution.
5. Rebuild the affected section from the evidence trail.
6. Apply the relevant fix for each failed check.
7. Ask for clarification when the source cannot resolve the issue.
8. Run the acceptance review again and record the result.

Not every problem is solved by rereading the source. The source can help with meaning and evidence, while the brief, accessibility standards, or content owner may be needed for voice, disclosure, or audience decisions.

The goal is not to make every output sound identical to its source. The goal is to make permitted changes deliberately while keeping unsupported claims out of the final work.
