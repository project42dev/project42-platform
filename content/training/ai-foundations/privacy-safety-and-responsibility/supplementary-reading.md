# Privacy, Safety, and Responsible Use

The video segments introduce responsible use as a practical part of working with AI. This reading adds a way to turn those ideas into everyday decisions. You do not need to memorize every possible risk. Instead, build a habit of asking a few questions before, during, and after AI use.

## Start with the smallest useful input

An AI system often does not need the original material. It may only need a description of the task, a few relevant facts, or a fictional example.

For example, suppose you want help improving a customer-service response. The system may need to know that a customer is waiting for a delayed appointment and wants an accessible option. It probably does not need the customer’s name, phone number, exact appointment date, or a private medical detail.

This practice is called **data minimization**: use only the information needed for the task. Minimization lowers the chance of accidental disclosure and makes it easier to review what was shared.

Removing names is helpful but not always sufficient. A person may still be identifiable from a combination of details, such as a rare job, a small town, an unusual event, and an exact date. Before entering information, ask:

- Could someone recognize the person from these details?
- Does the task really require this detail?
- Can I replace it with a role, range, summary, or fictional example?
- Would I be comfortable explaining this input to the person affected?

Accessibility information deserves special care. A general statement such as “the organization should explain accessible entrances” can be useful and appropriate. A detailed account of one recognizable person’s mobility needs may be private. Protect the individual while preserving the general accessibility information people need.

## Decide how much review is needed

A useful risk check considers **consequence, exposure, and reversibility**.

- **Consequence:** What harm could result if the output is wrong?
- **Exposure:** Who could see or act on it?
- **Reversibility:** How difficult would it be to correct the result? A low-reversibility task is hard to undo.

These factors do not always rise together. A private recommendation can have high consequences but limited exposure. A public announcement can have wide exposure but low consequences if it contains a minor typo. Consider each factor separately.

For a low-risk draft, a person may perform a quick factual and privacy check. For a public or consequential result, add stronger safeguards: minimize the input, restrict access, compare the output with trusted information, involve a qualified reviewer, record the decision, and define how to correct errors. AI should assist with high-impact decisions, not quietly become the final decision-maker.

## Check more than factual accuracy

An answer can be factually plausible and still be unsafe.

**Privacy** asks whether personal or confidential information was exposed or could be inferred. **Security** asks whether content could manipulate the task, reveal protected information, or cause someone to take an unsafe action. Treat instructions found inside uploaded or retrieved material as content to examine, not automatically as instructions to follow.

**Bias** can appear when examples or source data reflect unequal treatment. Review results across relevant groups, languages, abilities, and situations. Neutral-sounding language can still produce unfair outcomes if it relies on stereotypes or performs poorly for one group.

**Transparency** means people should know when AI contributed to a result and where human judgment was used. Do not present an unverified AI answer as though it came from an authoritative source.

**Misuse** considers what someone could do with the output. A request that sounds harmless could support impersonation, harassment, privacy invasion, or bypassing a safeguard. Think about likely downstream use, not just the immediate wording.

## Make accountability visible

A responsible-use plan should name roles, not just intentions. One role can own the task. Another can verify facts or policy. A reviewer can check accessibility and respectful language. A human role should approve publication or another consequential action.

Plans also need monitoring. Look for complaints, corrections, unusual output patterns, privacy concerns, and differences in performance across groups. Monitoring is especially important when conditions change or when people rely on the output repeatedly.

Finally, plan for recovery. Decide who can pause or remove an output, how an error will be assessed, how affected people will be notified when appropriate, and what will be recorded. If personal information is shared accidentally, stop further use, notify the responsible privacy or security role, preserve only necessary evidence, and follow the organization’s incident process.

Responsible use is therefore a cycle: limit the input, assess the stakes, verify the result, assign ownership, monitor what happens, and recover when needed.
