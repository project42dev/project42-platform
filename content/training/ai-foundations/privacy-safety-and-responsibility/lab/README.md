# Lab: Build a Responsible-Use Plan for an AI-Assisted Customer FAQ

## Objective

Apply the module’s concepts to a realistic scenario. You will create:

- An input boundary
- An anonymized source
- A consequence, exposure, and reversibility assessment
- A verification checklist
- An ownership plan
- A monitoring and recovery plan

## Scenario

A community organization wants to use an AI system to help turn customer-service notes into a public FAQ. The notes may include questions about delayed appointments, billing confusion, accessibility needs, and complaints.

The final FAQ will be published on a public website. It must not identify individual customers, expose confidential information, or make unsupported promises.

Use these fictional notes:

> Several people asked why appointments moved from Tuesday to Thursday. One person said they could not use the stairs and needed an accessible entrance. A customer mentioned that a payment was reversed after a billing error. Another person included a private phone number and asked for a call back. A staff member wrote that a particular customer is “always difficult.”

## Prerequisites

- The scenario above
- A text editor or worksheet
- Basic understanding of privacy, consequence, exposure, reversibility, bias, and verification
- An AI system is optional. You may complete the lab manually or use an approved system with fictional content only.

**Safety rule:** Do not enter real customer information, contact details, account information, or confidential organizational material.

## Instructions

### 1. Separate useful content from sensitive content

Create two lists:

- Information that could help answer a general public question
- Information that must not be entered into an AI system or published as written

Consider names, phone numbers, exact customer circumstances, judgments about individuals, and accessibility-related details.

Make an important distinction:

- A **general accessibility topic**, such as “the FAQ should explain accessible entrances,” may be retained and generalized.
- An **individual’s identifiable accessibility circumstance**, especially when combined with unusual details, should be removed or generalized.

A judgment such as “always difficult” should not be treated as a fact about a person. It should be removed or rewritten as an observable service issue, if the issue is relevant.

**If you are unsure:** Treat the specific detail as sensitive, but preserve the useful general topic. Rewrite it without identifying or judgmental information.

**Expected result:** A classification list with at least three safe-to-generalize items and three items requiring removal or protection.

### 2. Write the input boundary

Complete this sentence:

> For this FAQ task, I may enter ____________________. I must not enter ____________________. Before sharing information, I will ____________________.

Include rules for:

- Personal data and contact details
- Confidential business information
- Unsupported claims
- Details that could identify a person
- General accessibility information that is useful to the public

**If the boundary is vague:** Replace words such as “private” or “important” with examples and clear actions. State that accessibility topics may be retained when generalized and non-identifying.

**Expected result:** A boundary that another beginner could follow without guessing.

### 3. Minimize and transform the source notes

Rewrite the notes as anonymized topic summaries. For example, turn the appointment-specific event into a general question about appointment changes.

Do not include:

- The private phone number
- An identifiable customer story
- The staff member’s judgment
- Details that are not needed for a public FAQ

You may retain a neutral, general accessibility topic, such as the need to explain accessible entrances.

**If your rewrite still identifies someone:** Remove exact dates, locations, unusual combinations of facts, and opinions about a named or recognizable person. If removing a detail would erase a useful accessibility topic, keep the topic but state it generally.

**Expected result:** Three to five neutral topics suitable for drafting a public FAQ.

### 4. Assess consequence, exposure, and reversibility

First complete the assessment columns. Use low, medium, or high ratings and explain each rating briefly. For reversibility, state whether the result is easy or difficult to undo.

| Task or output | Consequence if wrong | Exposure | Reversibility | Reason for ratings |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Explaining appointment changes |  |  |  |  |
| Describing accessibility options |  |  |  |  |
| Explaining billing corrections |  |  |  |  |
| Publishing the final FAQ |  |  |  |  |

After completing the ratings, add a separate safeguard decision:

| Task or output | Safeguard selected after assessment |
|---|---|
| Explaining appointment changes |  |
| Describing accessibility options |  |
| Explaining billing corrections |  |
| Publishing the final FAQ |  |

Possible safeguards include a fact check against current policy, accessibility review, privacy review, restricted input, human approval, and a correction plan. A public FAQ normally requires stronger review than an internal brainstorming draft.

**If ratings are inconsistent:** Ask what could happen to a person, how many people could see or act on the result, and whether the result could be corrected after publication. Then choose safeguards only after revising the ratings.

**Expected result:** Two linked tables showing that stronger or less-reversible work receives stronger safeguards.

### 5. Draft a verification checklist

Create at least five checks. Include:

- Factual accuracy
- Privacy and re-identification
- Accessibility and respectful language
- Unsupported promises or invented policy
- Bias or unfair assumptions
- Whether a human owner approved publication

Give each check an outcome such as **pass**, **revise**, or **escalate**.

**If a check cannot be tested:** Rewrite it as an observable question, such as “Can we confirm this statement in the organization’s current policy?”

**Expected result:** A checklist with a clear outcome for every item.

### 6. Assign ownership

Name roles rather than real people. Assign responsibility for:

- Approving the input boundary
- Reviewing the AI-assisted draft
- Confirming policy and billing claims
- Checking accessibility language
- Approving publication
- Monitoring feedback
- Responding to an incident

Publication approval must belong to a human role. Do not assign final approval to the AI system.

**If no role seems appropriate:** Use a role such as service owner, communications reviewer, accessibility reviewer, or privacy contact. Clarify who has authority to pause publication.

**Expected result:** An ownership table with one accountable role for each activity.

### 7. Define monitoring and recovery

Write a short plan answering:

- What signals will be monitored after publication?
- How can someone report an error or privacy concern?
- Who can pause or remove the FAQ?
- How will a correction be approved?
- What will be recorded after an incident?

For accidental publication of personal information, include this sequence:

1. Pause or remove the affected content.
2. Notify the responsible privacy or security role.
3. Assess what was exposed and who may be affected.
4. Correct the FAQ and decide whether notification is appropriate.
5. Record the incident, decision, and lesson learned.

**If the plan only says “fix it quickly”:** Replace it with the sequence above and name the responsible roles.

**Expected result:** A recovery plan that can be followed under time pressure.

### 8. Review the finished plan

Check your work against the learning objectives:

- Did you identify sensitive data?
- Did you set an input boundary before using an AI system?
- Did safeguards increase with consequence, exposure, or reduced reversibility?
- Did you address privacy, security, bias, transparency, and misuse?
- Did you assign ownership for verification, monitoring, and recovery?

**If any answer is no:** Return to the relevant step and revise before considering the lab complete.

## Expected final output

Submit one document containing:

1. Data classification
2. Input boundary
3. Anonymized topic summaries
4. The assessment table
5. The post-assessment safeguard table
6. Verification checklist
7. Ownership table
8. Monitoring and recovery plan

A successful submission protects individuals, preserves useful general accessibility information, avoids unsupported claims, makes human responsibility visible, and provides a practical response when something goes wrong.

## Troubleshooting

- **You cannot decide whether a detail is sensitive:** Exclude the specific detail and explain the task at a more general level. Preserve a non-identifying general topic when it is useful.
- **You are about to remove all accessibility information:** Keep the public-facing need, such as information about accessible entrances, but remove the individual’s identifiable circumstances.
- **The AI system produces a confident but unsupported answer:** Mark the claim for human verification; do not publish it as fact.
- **The output includes a person’s circumstances:** Remove the detail, regenerate or rewrite the draft, and check for indirect identification.
- **The output uses judgmental language:** Replace labels with observable, neutral descriptions and ask an appropriate reviewer to assess the wording.
- **The output is useful but too broad for publication:** Restrict the audience, shorten the content, and require a human approval step.
- **You discover that real data was entered accidentally:** Stop using the output, notify the responsible privacy or security role, preserve only necessary evidence, and follow the organization’s incident process.
